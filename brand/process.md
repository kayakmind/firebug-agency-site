# The Firebug brand process

How Firebug takes a client from "we need a brand" (or "our brand needs to catch up") to a
working identity and the system behind it. Written while running the process on Firebug itself,
October 2026. This is the internal version: it names where AI does the work. The client-facing
version names the benefit (speed, range, more options) and never the mechanism.

Status: living document. Stages 1 and 2 are complete for Firebug and are documented as run.
Stage 3 is in progress. Stages 4 and 5 are drafted from how identity firms work and will be
revised once we have run them.

---

## Shape of an engagement

| Stage | Output | Typical time, traditional agency | Firebug |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Discovery | Brief, audit, questions answered | 2 to 4 weeks | Days |
| 1. Positioning | Positioning statement, onliness, audience, rules-out | 2 to 3 weeks | A week |
| 2. Messaging platform | Headline stack, core messages, offer structure, beliefs, voice | 2 to 3 weeks | A week |
| 3. Visual identity | Principles, territories, chosen system, applications, guidelines | 6 to 10 weeks | 2 to 3 weeks |
| 4. Build | Website and the first set of applications | 6 to 12 weeks | 2 to 4 weeks |
| 5. Rollout | Launch sequence, templates, governance, handoff | 2 to 4 weeks | A week |

Clients can enter at any stage ("the brand, the story, the reach"). A client with a sound
positioning and a tired identity starts at stage 3; stages 1 and 2 become a short check
rather than a rebuild.

The method at every stage is the same: Firebug does the thinking in dialogue with the client,
one decision at a time, with options and a recommendation; AI does the production between
decisions (research sweeps, comparison tables, draft copy, variant generation, mockups). The
client sees decisions, not drafts.

---

## Stage 0: Discovery

Purpose: understand the organization well enough to write its positioning.

**Inputs we ask for.** Existing brand materials (guidelines, logo files, site, deck, recent
posts), the last twelve months of comms, the founder or leadership's own description of the
organization, who they consider competitors, who they admire, what's on the calendar (launch,
round, season, announcement).

**What we do.**
- Audit what exists: what the current brand says, where it contradicts itself, what's
  salvageable. (AI: sweep every public surface, extract claims, flag drift.)
- Landscape: how 8 to 15 comparable organizations describe themselves, verbatim; how their
  sites are structured; what they claim and what they don't. (AI: fetch, quote, table.)
- Stakeholder conversation: one or two sessions with the people who own the brand. Questions
  are about purpose, audience, the moment, what "winning" looks like, what they would never say.
- Benefits over features: for every service or product, the benefit ladder (feature, functional
  benefit, emotional or strategic benefit).

**Output.** A short brief: what the organization is, who it serves, what moment it's in, what
the brand has to do, and the open questions stage 1 must answer.

## Stage 1: Positioning

Purpose: one paragraph that everything else is derived from.

**The nine decisions, in order.** Each gates the next.
1. Who it's for (audience, defined by moment or disposition, not demographics)
2. The category (the noun a prospect files you under)
3. The core promise (what the client gets, in their terms)
4. The differentiator (what you deliver that the alternatives can't; the hardest one)
5. The name story (what the name and mark already mean, and whether to say it)
6. Reasons to believe (proof)
7. What's out (the explicit non-offers that make the offer credible)
8. Voice (temperature and edge)
9. How the mechanism appears (for Firebug: AI unnamed; for a client: whatever their "how" is)

**Method.** One decision per exchange. Two to four options, trade-offs stated, a
recommendation. The client's answer is recorded before the next question. A running decision
file is kept from the first exchange (for Firebug: `positioning-notes.md`).

**Output.** Positioning statement (internal, never published verbatim), onliness statement
("the only ___ that ___"), the three-way claim if there is one, rules-out, phrases to avoid.

**Tests.** The onliness statement has to be true, specific, and something a competitor could
not say. Read every line as two different clients; if either winces, rewrite.

## Stage 2: Messaging platform

Purpose: the words the brand says, in order of altitude.

**Six sections, presented and approved one at a time.**
1. Positioning and onliness (carried in from stage 1)
2. The headline stack: tagline, claim line, the one argument, the promise; each with payoff copy
3. Core messages: three pillars, each a benefit, each answering a fear, each with a proof slot
4. The offer: organized by the client's moment or outcome, disciplines underneath; the
   organizing idea for the services page
5. Beliefs: three or four statements for the About page; a worldview, not a résumé
6. Voice and vocabulary: traits made operational, rules, words used and avoided, the two-reader test

**Plus the plain answer:** one sentence and one paragraph that say what the organization does
with no metaphor and no spark. For directories, LinkedIn, proposals, any conversational
interface.

**Method.** Research first: how comparable organizations structure their own messaging and
their sites (nav, hero, how services are grouped, proof, people, entry offer). Then draft each
section in the client's voice, show it, revise on their pushback, record approval.

**Output.** The platform folded into the brand guidelines, with a map of where each piece lands
on the site.

## Stage 3: Visual identity

Purpose: a system, not a logo. The logo may already exist (Firebug's did).

**3.1 Design principles.** Three or four short lines that translate the positioning into
visual instructions. Every later choice is checked against them. Firebug's: Heat, not warmth.
Ink on a surface. One hot thing per screen. Senior, not slick.

**3.2 Territories.** Two or three complete visual worlds, each obeying the principles
differently: palette, type pairing, imagery idea, layout feel, shown as a moodboard plus a
sample application (usually the home page hero). The client picks a world or a hybrid.
(AI: generate the worlds as browser screens in hours, not weeks.)

**3.3 The system**, element by element, inside the chosen world, in this order. Each element is
one screen with one question and a stated recommendation, so the client can answer in a word.

1. Color: the ground, then the ink, then the accent. The ink generates the neutral scale, so it
   is decided before anything is drawn in it. Then the usage rules: proportion, accent budget
   per page, dark surfaces.
2. Typography: faces and roles, then the full scale set in real copy at phone and desktop sizes.
3. Logo system: mark, wordmark, lockups, clear space, minimum sizes, color versions, misuse,
   and the list of files to produce. The mark is measured in its own unit so every rule is a
   ratio, not a pixel.
4. Graphic devices and grid: the rules, the motifs, the grid, the spacing scale, buttons, links,
   fields, how images sit, and a don't list.
5. Imagery: illustration or photography language. Motion, if any, is explored on its own after
   the system is set, with a still fallback.

**3.4 Applications.** The system on real things: home page screens, a social post, a deck
cover and inside page, a proposal page, an email signature, a card. This is the test, and the
pass criterion is explicit: nothing on the sheet needed a rule the system didn't already have.
What fails goes back to 3.3. Small conventions that emerge (template variants, where the page
number sits) are recorded, not treated as failures.

**3.5 Guidelines and assets.** Brand guidelines rewritten; logo and template files regenerated
from a single master (a script, not a folder of hand-edited files), so a change to the mark is
one edit. A decision record sits beside the guidelines: every choice, what it was chosen over,
and why.

**Method notes.**
- Explore whole worlds before refining single variables. Comparing four reds on one screen is
  refinement; it comes after the world is chosen, not before. (Firebug's own run began with
  single-variable comparisons and had to step back to principles and territories. The order
  matters more than the speed.)
- Every screen is a private web page the client can open on a phone. Decisions happen between
  meetings, in a reply of one word.
- Stage 3 for Firebug, from principles to applications with guidelines and assets, took one
  working day of dialogue. The equivalent at a traditional firm is six to ten weeks.

---

## Stage 4: Build

Purpose: the website and the first applications, in the system.

- Sitemap: pages and what each one is for, before any page is designed
- Wireframes per page, approved one at a time
- Design in the system
- Build (for Firebug: Next.js on Vercel), with the brand tokens as code
- Content loaded from the messaging platform, not rewritten

---

## Stage 5: Rollout

Purpose: the brand keeps working without us in the room.

- Launch sequence across channels (site, social, email, press if any), on a date
- Templates: social, deck, document, email
- Governance: who approves what, how new material is checked against the guidelines
- Handoff: files, tokens, guidelines, a short walkthrough

---

## What this looks like from the client's side

Firebug's offer language (from the messaging platform): "We can take on all three, or start
where you need us most." The stages above are the three levels in sequence (the brand: stages
0 to 3; the story: stages 2 and 4; the reach: stages 4 and 5). A client is shown stages and
decisions, never the production between them.

Entry point: a free 30-minute conversation. First deliverable for a full engagement: the stage
0 brief.
